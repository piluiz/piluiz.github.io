Sleep Right

spring 2023 · usc ee 459 · #embedded-c #atmega328p

Sleep Right is a wearable prototype that wakes you at a lighter point in your sleep cycle, instead of at a fixed time. It was my senior design capstone for USC's EE 459Lx Embedded Systems Design Laboratory, built with a team of three. I wrote most of the firmware, from heart-rate processing and sleep-stage detection to the state machine that runs the device.

Overview

The semester’s design theme was a smart device that benefits the environment, and health counted toward it. We picked sleep: waking up during deep sleep leaves you groggy no matter how long you slept, while waking from light or REM sleep feels much easier.

Sleep Right stays in a low-power mode for most of the night. Starting 90 minutes before your alarm, it reads your pulse to estimate which sleep stage you’re in. If it sees you move from light sleep into REM, it wakes you right then with a vibration motor instead of a loud alarm. If that never happens, it wakes you at the alarm time.

My part


    

  • State machine: setting the time and alarm with the knob and buttons, low-power sleep, pulse monitoring, and the wake-up alarm
    • 

  • Heart rate: the pulse sensor triggers an interrupt on each beat, and the time between beats gives the BPM
    • 

  • Cleaning the data: filtering out noisy readings and smoothing the BPM with running averages
    • 

  • Sleep-stage detection: estimating the stage from heart-rate trends, and taking extra readings to confirm a light-to-REM transition before waking anyone up
    • 



Hardware


The prototype runs on an ATmega328P on a soldered protoboard. The real-time clock and the 4×20 LCD share a single I2C bus to save pins, and we checked the traffic with an oscilloscope. Four AA batteries power the board, and a coin cell keeps the clock running if they die. We also planned how to shrink it into a watch: a small PCB, a single coin-cell battery with a boost converter, and a smaller display.


Takeaways


The prototype worked, but it also showed us how far the idea was from a real product. Turning a protoboard, AA batteries, and a 4×20 LCD into something you could comfortably wear all night, like a Garmin watch or a sleep ring, was the hard part. We only got as far as planning a smaller watch version.


Heart rate alone also isn’t enough to reliably tell sleep stages apart. A real product would combine more signals, like an accelerometer to detect movement and blood oxygen (SpO2) levels, with algorithms that adapt to each person’s sleep patterns.
